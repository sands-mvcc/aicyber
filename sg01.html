Interactive Lesson: Mapping AI Objectives to Traditional Cyber Principles

Lesson Context: Bridging Security+ and AI

The Core Premise: Security+ provides foundational governance, risk, architecture, and incident-response concepts. However, AI introduces novel challenges that require extending these foundations.

  • What remains the same: The need for policy, oversight, and defense-in-depth architecture.
  • What AI adds: New policy demands, specialized lifecycles, regulatory frameworks, and complex behavioral-control mechanisms (e.g., prompt injection, model drift).

Instructions: Review the mapping below. Use the interactive toggles to explore the rationale behind each domain alignment and understand why traditional controls only partially cover AI risks.

Explain why traditional certifications alone are insufficient for AI-enabled enterprises. Governance, Risk & Compliance Partially covered
Why this is right: AI-specific governance frameworks, risk categories, and lifecycle controls extend rather than replace traditional security. Because it centers on policy, oversight, and formal risk treatment, GRC is the best fit.
Identify the major AI governance frameworks (NIST AI RMF, ISO 42001, EU AI Act). Governance, Risk & Compliance Partially covered
Why this is right: Frameworks fundamentally belong to GRC. While Security+ covers standard frameworks (like NIST CSF), AI introduces specialized regulatory demands.
Apply the four NIST AI RMF functions to enterprise AI deployment. Governance, Risk & Compliance Partially covered
Why this is right: The four functions of the NIST AI RMF are Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. These are inherently risk and compliance functions, though they extend into operational territory.
Classify AI systems under the EU AI Act's four risk tiers. Governance, Risk & Compliance Not covered
Why this is right: Traditional cyber certs do not cover AI-specific legislation. The EU AI Act uses a four-tier risk approach: unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal risk. This is a pure GRC objective.
Differentiate AI risk categories: model risk, data risk, operational risk. Governance, Risk & Compliance Partially covered
Why this is right: Security+ introduces standard risk thinking, but AI model risk, adversarial risk, and ethical/societal risk do not map cleanly onto traditional CIA-triad thinking.
Recognize shadow AI patterns and apply governance responses. Governance, Risk & Compliance Partially covered
Why this is right: While shadow IT is a known concept, "shadow AI governance responses" center heavily on establishing new corporate policy and oversight rules, placing it firmly in GRC.
Conduct vendor and supply-chain risk assessments for AI solutions. Governance, Risk & Compliance Partially covered
Why this is right: Security+ addresses third-party risk generally. However, the AI supply chain adds opacity, model dependencies, and inherited risks from external models and services, necessitating an updated approach.
Design a defense-in-depth architecture for an AI-enabled application. Architecture & Implementation Partially covered
Why this is right: Defense-in-depth belongs directly to Architecture & Implementation. Still only "partially covered" because AI introduces novel architectural threats such as prompt injection that require new layers of defense.
Adapt NIST SP 800-61 incident response lifecycle for AI-specific incidents. Incident Response Partially covered
Why this is right: Adapting the 800-61 lifecycle belongs closely to IR. It is only partially covered because traditional IR does not account for AI novelties like model drift and behavioral monitoring during an incident.
